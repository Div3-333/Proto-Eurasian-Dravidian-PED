\section{The Epistemological Horizon of Comparative Philology}
Traditional comparative linguistics, since its formalization by the Neogrammarians in the 19th century, has been anchored by the \textit{Stammbaumtheorie} (family tree model). While this model has successfully reconstructed Proto-Indo-European (PIE) and Proto-Dravidian (PD) back to approximately 6,000–8,000 BP, it hits a seemingly impenetrable barrier at the 10,000-year mark. The fundamental problem is entropy: the gradual loss of phonetic and semantic signal over time.

\section{The Failure of Traditional Long-Range Comparison}
Mainstream historical linguistics has rightfully viewed macro-family reconstructions (e.g., Nostratic, Eurasiatic) with extreme skepticism. The primary culprit is the 'look-alike' fallacy: the tendency for researchers to scan vast dictionaries for words that sound similar and share vague semantic links (e.g., 'fire' and 'burn'). 

\subsection{Case Study: The Apophenia of Similarity}
Consider the often-cited match between the English 'bad' and the Persian 'bad.' Despite the perfect phonetic and semantic alignment, these words are entirely unrelated etymologically—the English 'bad' derives from Old English \textit{bæddel}, while the Persian word has a distinct Indo-Iranian origin. Given a large enough dataset (thousands of dialects across three continents), such matches are statistically inevitable and mathematically meaningless. To claim genetic relationship based on similarity is to mistake statistical noise for a signal.

    \item \textbf{Data Purging:} All semantic labels, translations, and glosses were purged from the Mahadevan/Wells corpus (4,968 inscriptions).
    \item \textbf{Categorical Encoding:} The script was reduced to a purely structural sequence of numeric sign IDs.
    \item \textbf{Train/Test Split:} The corpus was divided into a Training Set (80\%) and a Held-Out Validation Set (20\%). 
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hidden Markov Model (HMM) Architecture}
We utilized a \textbf{CategoricalHMM} with four syntactic states (S0–S3), representing the hypothesized Active-Stative-Verb-Terminal roles. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baum-Welch Estimation:} The transition matrix \textbf{A} and emission matrix \textbf{B} were learned iteratively via expectation-maximization.
    \item \textbf{Laplacian Smoothing:} To handle rare signs in the test set, we applied additive smoothing ($\epsilon = 1e-6$).
\end{itemize}

\part{Synthesis and Context}
\chapter{Archaeological Stratigraphy: The IUP Horizon}

\section{The Material Proxy for PED Radiation}
Historical linguistics cannot exist in a vacuum. To validate a 40,000-year-old reconstruction, we must identify a corresponding material radiation in the archaeological record. We identify the \textbf{Initial Upper Paleolithic (IUP)} lithic complex (c. 45,000–35,000 BP) as the definitive material proxy for the Proto-Eurasian-Dravidian radiation. This period marks a fundamental shift from Middle Paleolithic flake technologies to the sophisticated, modular blade-and-point assemblages that define the Upper Paleolithic.

\section{The Altai-Pamir Interaction Sphere}
The 'Pamir-Altai Corridor' served as the geographic hub where the PED mother tongue achieved its structural maturity. We identify two primary anchor sites:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kara-Bom (Altai Mountains):} Excavations here document a continuous transition from Levallois-like techniques to specialized blade production c. 43,000 BP. This site represents the 'Phonological Laboratory' of the ancestral speakers.
    \item \textbf{Obi-Rakhmat (Tian Shan):} A critical site documenting the intersection of Siberian and Western Asian lithic traditions, providing the technological diversity that matches the PED morphological complexity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Micro-Blade Dispersal: The Triple Radiation}
The stratigraphic evidence shows a rapid dispersal of IUP technologies along three primary axes, matching the linguistic divergence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Eastern Axis (Sino-Tibetan):} Dispersal into the Tibetan Plateau and the Yellow River basin (e.g., Shuidonggou site, c. 38,000 BP).
    \item \textbf{The Southern Axis (Dravidian):} Dispersal toward the Indian subcontinent via the Pamir-Hindu Kush interface (e.g., Jwalapuram evidence).
    \item \textbf{The Western Axis (Indo-European):} Dispersal into the Pontic-Caspian steppe and Eastern Europe (e.g., Bacho Kiro cave, c. 45,000 BP).
