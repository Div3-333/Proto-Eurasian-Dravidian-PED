\chapter{Methodological Framework: The Adversarial Protocol}

\section{The Epistemological Horizon of Comparative Philology}
Traditional comparative linguistics, since its formalization by the Neogrammarians in the 19th century, has been anchored by the \textit{Stammbaumtheorie} (family tree model). While this model has successfully reconstructed Proto-Indo-European (PIE) and Proto-Dravidian (PD) back to approximately 6,000–8,000 BP, it hits a seemingly impenetrable barrier at the 10,000-year mark. The fundamental problem is entropy: the gradual loss of phonetic and semantic signal over time.

\section{The Failure of Traditional Long-Range Comparison}
Mainstream historical linguistics has rightfully viewed macro-family reconstructions (e.g., Nostratic, Eurasiatic) with extreme skepticism. The primary culprit is the 'look-alike' fallacy: the tendency for researchers to scan vast dictionaries for words that sound similar and share vague semantic links (e.g., 'fire' and 'burn'). 

\subsection{Case Study: The Apophenia of Similarity}
Consider the often-cited match between the English 'bad' and the Persian 'bad.' Despite the perfect phonetic and semantic alignment, these words are entirely unrelated etymologically—the English 'bad' derives from Old English \textit{bæddel}, while the Persian word has a distinct Indo-Iranian origin. Given a large enough dataset (thousands of dialects across three continents), such matches are statistically inevitable and mathematically meaningless. To claim genetic relationship based on similarity is to mistake statistical noise for a signal.

\section{The Adversarial Philology Protocol}
To overcome the trap of confirmation bias, this project implemented the \textbf{Adversarial Philology Protocol}. Throughout the research cycle, a specialized 'Brutal Reviewer' (Reviewer 2) was tasked with dismantling the hypothesis at every stage. This forced the research to pivot from speculative pattern-matching to predictive structural modeling.

\subsection{Methodological Evolution through Friction}
An early iteration of the PED hypothesis was rejected for relying too heavily on 'Proto-to-Proto' comparison. The reviewer argued that comparing two theoretical reconstructions (e.g., PIE and Proto-Dravidian) merely compounds the errors inherent in each theory. This friction forced a fundamental shift to \textbf{Primary Data Anchoring}, using only the most archaic attested strata of Vedic Sanskrit, Old Tamil, and Old Tibetan as our foundational data points.

\section{The Non-Linear Signal Constraint}
We established a strict constraint: no lexical item is accepted as evidence unless it follows a non-linear phonetic shift. A valid PED root must exhibit the \textbf{Glottalic Shift Matrix}. This shift requires a 'triangulation' of three distinct phonetic outcomes, making the probability of accidental alignment vanishingly small.

\section{The Zero-Shot Verification Standard}
Finally, the project adheres to the \textbf{Zero-Shot Verification Standard}. Our models are not merely tested on how well they fit known data, but on how well they predict the structure of \textit{unseen} data. As documented in Chapter 7, our Hidden Markov Model (HMM) was trained on 80\% of the Indus Valley Script corpus and tasked with predicting the transitions of the remaining 20\% held-out set. The result—a decisive log-likelihood advantage—constitutes the 'External Witness' required for scientific acceptance.
